# Pijul and Darcs

> Darcs is a distributed VCS in Haskell by David Roundy — a physicist (PhD Berkeley 2001; Oregon State physics faculty 2006–2022) — which "evolved out of David Roundy's efforts to design a new patch format for GNU arch in June 2002" and was released publicly in April 2003. It is the canonical *patch-based* system: a repository is a set of patches, and its "theory of patches" defines commutation, inversion and merge algebraically. It is still maintained (2.18.5, 9 January 2025) but has been ecosystem-marginal since GHC — its flagship user — migrated to Git around 31 March 2011. Pijul is its theoretical successor in Rust by Pierre-Étienne Meunier and Florent Becker; the `pijul` crate was first published on crates.io on 2 February 2016. It replaces Darcs' ad-hoc conflict machinery with an implementation of Mimram & Di Giusto's categorical model, where merge is a pushout and conflicting states are the free finite cocompletion that makes all pushouts exist. Pijul reached `1.0.0-alpha` on 9 November 2020, 1.0-beta on 18 January 2022, and is at `1.0.0-beta.22` (published 23 August 2026) — still beta, more than ten years after first publication.

## 1. What did it get right?

### 1.1 Darcs: change identity is independent of the tree it lands on

Roundy's core move is that a patch names a *change*, not a diff-against-a-parent. Commutation `P₂P₁ ↔ P₁'P₂'` rewrites the representation of the same two changes into a different order; `P₁'` is a different byte-level hunk from `P₁` (offsets move) but is the *same change*. Merge reduces to commutation. This is the origin of the property Lattice's spec calls "changes carry stable IDs surviving amendment," and it is why Darcs' cherry-pick composes: pulling *X* from a branch and later pulling *Y* from the same branch works, because *X* did not change identity on arrival. Pijul's FAQ states the contrast directly — in Git, cherry-picking rebases commits onto the current branch, "losing their identity," which "causes artificial conflicts if you cherry-pick again between the same branches."

Ganesh Sittampalam et al.'s *Some properties of darcs patch theory* (7 November 2005) axiomatises this with five assumptions: associativity of patch sequencing, uniqueness of commutation, invertibility of commutation, three-way permutivity of commutation, and consistency of failure. Footnote 4, attached to Theorem 2, concedes that the converse of the patch-correspondence theorem probably fails: "However I think it would require some kind of stronger property, such as unique patch ids, to make it so." Two cautions the prior draft suppressed. The footnote offers unique patch ids as *an example* of a sufficient strengthening, not as the identified answer; and the document is an informal working note, not a published proof — its own footnotes read "This is horrible. I need a better notation" and "More rabbits out of hats." Lattice's stable change IDs are consistent with this hint. They are not licensed by it.

### 1.2 Darcs: merges cannot fail

Merge is total. Where commutation is undefined, Darcs 1 produced a *merger* patch; Darcs 2 (April 2008) replaced these with *conflictors* (`RepoPatchV2`, named `RealPatch` before 2.12), which store exactly three things: the original primitive change `p`; the transitive closure of changes `p` conflicts with that conflict with nothing else; and the changes `p` conflicts with that *do* conflict with something else. The payoff is structural: the conflict is a value in history, so unpulling one side automatically un-conflicts the other. Lattice's "conflicts are first-class objects inside checkpoints carrying both sides + base" is the same idea with a simpler carrier.

The Darcs wiki also records the cost, which Lattice should read as a warning rather than a footnote: conflictors "must correctly track and update" their three components under commutation, "and it is this tracking of information which apparently has bugs" — concretely issue2047, where commuting a conflictor with a *duplicate* is not guaranteed to be invertible. Making conflicts first-class buys convergence; it does not buy correctness for free.

### 1.3 Pijul: a correctness criterion, not just an algorithm

Mimram & Di Giusto (arXiv:1311.3903, 13 November 2013) define a category **L** whose objects are files `A : [n] → L` (a finite sequence of lines) and whose morphisms are patches — injective increasing partial functions `f : [m] → [n]` with `B ∘ f(i) = A(i)` where defined. Merge is the pushout of two coinitial patches. Their observation is that **L** lacks some pushouts, and that this absence *is* the definition of conflict: "the diagram `acb ← ab → adb` does not admit a pushout in **L**. In this case, the two patches f₁ and f₂ are said to be conflicting." They then take the free finite conservative cocompletion **P** and derive a concrete description.

**Correction to the prior draft, and it is load-bearing.** **P**'s objects are *not* a partial order over lines. They are "finite sets labeled by lines together with a transitive relation," and the paper flags the reason explicitly: "perhaps a bit surprisingly, it turns out that we have to 'allow cycles' in the objects of the category P." A partial order is antisymmetric and therefore cannot represent the cyclic conflicts that actually occur (Pijul's directory-rename cycle is exactly one). Any Lattice conflict representation modelled on "a partial order over identified lines" is provably too weak for the conflict class the prior art already catalogued. The paper cites Darcs' mergers, conflictors and graphictors by name as the ad-hoc alternatives it replaces.

### 1.4 Pijul: the actual data structure

Pijul's repository state is a directed graph. Vertices are byte intervals identified by `(hash of the introducing change, byte range within that change)` — so two textually identical lines from different changes are distinct vertices, and "a line keeps its identity, even if the change is applied in a totally different context." There are exactly two operations: add vertices with alive edges around them, and map an existing edge label onto a different one. Edge labels are "fully described by two parameters: (1) their status (alive, deleted, and a few others…), and (2) the change that introduced them." The structure is append-only; deletion is a relabel.

Three mechanisms are worth stealing outright.

- **Edge labels carry the introducing change, and this is load-bearing.** The manual's counterexample: two authors delete the same line in parallel and one reverts. Without per-change labels "the two deletions get merged into one, and the inverse applies to both" — the single revert resurrects the line for the author who did not revert. This is exactly the bug a "last-writer-wins on a byte range" model produces.
- **Pseudo-edges.** "Every time we delete an edge, we add pseudo-edges between the source of the edge and all the descendants of the target," keeping the alive subgraph connected. Without them "the complexity of applying changes and outputting the repository would depend linearly on the size of the graph" — including deleted content. This is the mechanism that lets a change-based model escape Darcs' performance profile.
- **Two vertices per file: a name vertex and an inode vertex.** This "allows directory renames to commute with file renames, and file renames to commute with edits at the beginning of the file." Modelling a path as a string makes rename-vs-edit an artificial conflict; modelling identity separately from name does not.

Pijul also notes that line splitting "is done by the diff algorithm and is encoded in the changes, instead of being set in stone for all repositories," and speculates: "With a different diff algorithm, we could imagine splitting contents according to programming language structure." That is Lattice's derived-semantic-layer thesis, stated by Pijul, with the crucial difference that Pijul makes the split authoritative once recorded, while Lattice keeps bytes authoritative.

One nuance the prior draft missed. Pijul states that it "implements a conflict-free replicated datatype (CRDT)" and that it "would remain a CRDT with or without the design choices explained above about edge labels: for example, we could decide that the 'deleted' status has higher precedence. But as shown above, that wouldn't model conflicts accurately." Convergence and accurate conflict modelling are *separate* properties. A design can have the first and lack the second — and Lattice's blob-reference conflict carrier is exactly a design that gets convergence cheaply and must earn accuracy separately.

### 1.5 Pijul: order-independent state naming

If changes commute, a version is an unordered set, so its identifier must be order-independent. Pijul rejects XOR-of-hashes and every other linear function: "since the hashes are random, there is a high probability that any n hashes form an independent linear basis… The problem of forging a version identity becomes easy, as it is just a matter of solving a linear equation." The failure it names is adversarial: "servers could trick clients into accepting that versions are synchronised, even though they are not." Their scheme "comes from the discrete log problem" — applying hash `h` to version `V = e^v` yields `V^h = e^{v·h}` — and they note the resemblance to homomorphic hashing.

### 1.6 Pijul: storage engineered for the model

Pijul abandoned LMDB for Sanakirja, a `no_std` copy-on-write B-tree. Meunier's stated motivation is forking: "I needed to fork tables efficiently, without copying anything. This was especially important back then, when Pijul tables for small repositories often weighed dozens of megabytes." Forking is implemented "with an extra table storing reference counts of each page that is referenced at least twice." He reports Sanakirja beating LMDB "by 20%-50% on a use case similar to my most common load (storing graphs in Pijul)" — the author's own benchmark, not independently replicated. (The frequently repeated "O(log n) table forks" figure could not be sourced from the post or the crate documentation and has been dropped.)

### 1.7 Darcs: the property-testing discipline

The most transferable engineering lesson is in the Darcs 2.16.1 release notes (14 August 2020), announcing `darcs-3`, derived from Ian Lynagh's `camp`. It fixes long-outstanding consistency bugs (issue1401, issue2605) and "reduces the worst case asymptotic runtime for commutation and merging from exponential to merely quadratic in the number of patches involved." Then the sentence that matters, quoted exactly because the causality is easy to garble: "we have improved our test case generator for sequences of patches. It now generates all possible conflict scenarios. Since the new theory no longer has worst case exponential runtime, we can and did test all required properties and invariants with a large number of generated test cases (up to 100000)."

**The complexity of the merge implementation gates the verifiability of the merge implementation.** Exponential merge was not only a user-facing performance bug; it made the system unfuzzable, and therefore unverifiable, for eighteen years.

## 2. Why didn't it win (or why is it niche)?

**Technical cause 1 — the exponential merge, and two decades of it.** The Darcs FAQ answers "Is the exponential merge problem fixed yet?" with a flat "No" (answer stamped 2012-08-02): Darcs 2 "reduces the [number] of scenarios that will trigger an exponential merge," and "fixing the underlying patch theory problems will potentially take us a very long time." `darcs-3` finally fixes it — and as of the darcs 2.18.5 man page it is still "EXPERIMENTAL and new in version 2.16… included only as a technology preview and we do NOT recommend to use it for any serious work," with darcs-2 remaining the default format. That is eighteen years from the June 2002 design to a first *unstable* implementation without the blowup, and twenty-four years without a default-on one.

**Technical cause 2 — patch algebra fights history navigation.** Pijul's 1.0-beta post concedes that "since the early days of patch-based version control (and for example in Darcs), there has always been a trade-off between the ease of use and intuitiveness of patches and the navigability of snapshots," and introduces *tags* — "a compressed version of a repository at some point in time" whose "main use at the moment is to make it efficient to browse a particular state of a repository." A patch-algebraic system reintroduced snapshots because you cannot cheaply answer "what did the tree look like then" from a commuting set.

**Technical cause 3 — the Git bridge is genuinely hard for patch algebra, and remains unshipped.** In January 2022 Meunier wrote that "converting from Git to Pijul is easy, and so is converting from Pijul to Git. However, making these two conversions work together is tricky, since commits cannot be produced from patches independently from other patches applied to the repository." The failure mode he names: "if Pijul fails to recognise a commit `a` as coming from a patch `b` already applied to the repository… Pijul could import `a` a second time, creating a patch `c` with the same content as `b`, but with a different hash. The problem is that applying `b` and `c` in the same repo could create lots of unintelligible conflicts." He calls for "nice algorithmic solutions." The `pijul` crate still ships a `git` feature (a `git2`-backed importer) and the manual's command reference still lists no `git` command; no announcement of a working bidirectional round-trip was found in this audit. Treat that as *not found*, not as proof of absence.

**Ecosystem causes, which dominate.** GHC's own 2008 evaluation — by GHC maintainers, not critics — lists, in order: conflicts and merging ("essentially the workaround is to discard the history from the branch when merging, and use ordinary diff/patch tools"); speed (annotate and per-file log impractical, "we can't use Trac's darcs integration or darcsweb"); regular non-merge bugs; UI ("in a conflict there's no way to tell which two patches are conflicting with each other(!)"); and flaky Windows support. Their benchmark table shows `darcs annotate` at 47.080s — and failing with `Stack space overflow` — against git's 0.936s, and clone at 28.276s (failing with `Unapplicable patch`) against 0.580s. The 23 July 2008 IRC meeting concluded that "a change would be beneficial."

And then nothing happened for three years. A later note on the same page records that at ICFP'10 "an informal show of hands… showed that there was roughly a 50/50 split for and against a switch," and that "we have no immediate plans to switch." GHC moved to Git around 31 March 2011. **The strongest incumbency signal in this prior art is not that Darcs lost; it is that a project whose own maintainers documented catastrophic, quantified failures took three more years to move.** Switching costs beat measured defects.

**The adoption numbers.** crates.io, retrieved 3 September 2026: `pijul` 192,622 lifetime downloads, 1,877 in the trailing 90 days; `libpijul` 177,059 / 1,064; `jj-cli` (Jujutsu — snapshot-based, Git backend by default, first published on crates.io 16 July 2023) 139,333 / 25,621; `gix` 44,836,338 / 9,631,423. Jujutsu's recent rate is ~13.6× Pijul's, from a crate first published seven years later. Two caveats the prior draft omitted: `gix` is a *library*, so its number counts dependency resolutions and CI, not humans, and belongs in a different unit entirely; and cargo counts undercount `jj`, which is widely installed via Homebrew and nix. The direction of the comparison is robust; the multiple is not. This is the central Q2 finding, and it is not a story about mathematics.

**And the theory was never the user's problem.** Pijul's FAQ, from at least July 2020 through June 2023, asked "Do files merged by Pijul always have the correct semantic?" and answered: "No. Semantics depends on the particular language you're using, and Pijul doesn't know about them." (That entry has since been removed from the live FAQ; it is preserved in the archived snapshots cited below, and the live manual's *Why Pijul* page still says every change "should be tested and go through code review.") The thing users actually fear — a merge that compiles and is wrong — is untouched by order-independence, which is a syntactic guarantee about line positions. A 2020 FAQ entry is even blunter about the theory's role: "Category theory has certainly been an *inspiration* for Pijul, but categories are neither algorithms nor data structures in themselves."

## 3. What will Lattice do differently, concretely?

**ADR-1 resolves to a checkpoint DAG over Merkle directory trees plus structural three-way merge, with order-independence demoted from axiom to tested property.** The Git bridge requirement decides this. Pijul's stuck round-trip follows from the model — a commit is a function of the whole applied set while a patch is not — so Lattice's checkpoints are snapshots, `ltx sync` emits Git commits by tree comparison, and a Git commit imports as a checkpoint carrying `git_commit_id` in its provenance record. **ADR-1 must state in its decision text that Lattice buys Git round-trip fidelity by giving up native order-independence, and recovers it only where gate G2.1 certifies it.**

**Gate G2.1 — `LTX-MERGE` conflict-shape property suite.** A generator enumerating the conflict shapes the prior art actually catalogues: Pijul's three content-level types (two alive vertices with no directed path either way; two alive vertices with paths in both directions, i.e. a cycle; zombie vertices) and four file-level types (a file added into a concurrently deleted directory; one file renamed to two names; two files renamed to one name; a directory-rename cycle), plus parallel-delete-then-one-revert. Three properties, each restated to be well-typed for a snapshot merge:

1. **Commutativity, over a normal form, not raw bytes.** `normalise(merge(base,a,b)) == normalise(merge(base,b,a))`, where `normalise` maps a conflict object to its *unordered* set of side-blobs plus base. Asserting byte equality of merged trees is wrong: a merge that emits ordered conflict regions is legitimately side-ordered, so the naive assertion either fails on correct output or forces an arbitrary canonical side-order into the format.
2. **Grouping-independence, stated as Pijul states it.** Applying `{B,C}` to `A` in one operation yields the same checkpoint tree as applying `B` then `C` — this is Pijul's associativity claim, and the property whose violation the G/X example demonstrates in diff3. `merge(a, merge(b,c)) == merge(merge(a,b), c)` as written in the prior draft is ill-typed for three-way merge, because the three operands do not share a base; that formulation is withdrawn.
3. **Resolution-order independence.** Resolving a conflict object yields the same checkpoint regardless of which side arrived first.

Gate threshold: 100,000 generated cases, matching the number Darcs reports at the same discipline.

**Acceptance criterion replacing the prior draft's "sub-quadratic" requirement.** Darcs achieved *quadratic* in the number of patches involved and judged that testable; demanding sub-quadratic is stricter than the evidence and mis-parameterised besides — Lattice's three-way merge is bounded by the size of the two sides, not by history depth, so "conflicting-change count" is not its complexity parameter at all. The transplantable requirement is the CI budget that made Darcs' verification possible: **ADR-1 acceptance criteria state that a full G2.1 run of 10⁵ cases must complete within a fixed CI wall-clock budget (proposed: 20 minutes on the reference runner), and that the cost of resolving a checkpoint containing `k` accumulated unresolved conflict objects must be at worst quadratic in `k`.** Merge cost is a testability constraint, not a performance nicety.

**Pijul's G/X reshuffle becomes a named regression fixture.** Alice adds G above everything and then another A and B above that; Bob inserts X between the original A and B; "Git, SVN, and Mercurial will merge this example… with the relative positions of G and X swapped," and neither Git nor Pijul reports a conflict. It enters `LTX-MERGE`'s fixture set as `fixture/gx-reshuffle`, asserted identically under structural merge and under the line-merge fallback.

**Conflicts as first-class values: Pijul's shape, not Pijul's carrier.** A Lattice conflict object stores both sides plus base as content-addressed blob references, not as an algebraic patch type; a checkpoint containing conflicts is legal, syncable and publishable; resolution is a later checkpoint naming the conflict object it resolves. Un-conflicting is `ltx undo` on the operation that introduced the side — an op-log pointer move, not an inverse-merge computation. Darcs' conflictor bug history (issue2047) is the reason the carrier is deliberately dumb.

**Steal the identity mechanisms, and price them.**
1. **`entry_id`.** Every file and directory entry in the Merkle tree carries an intrinsic `entry_id` distinct from its path — Pijul's name-vertex/inode-vertex split, expressed in a snapshot tree — so rename-vs-edit and rename-vs-rename are distinguishable at merge time.
2. **Deletion provenance.** A deletion records the change that introduced the deleted content, so parallel-delete-then-one-revert does not resurrect content for the non-reverting party. Required G2.1 scenario. **Cost, stated because the prior draft did not: in a snapshot model this requires a per-range attribution index over live content — a blame index maintained incrementally, which is precisely the structure snapshot systems are bad at.** ADR-1 must either budget for that index or explicitly scope the guarantee to whole-entry deletions in v1.
3. **No linear set accumulators anywhere in `LTX-SYNC`.** Pijul's finding is that any linear function of hashes is forgeable by solving a linear equation, enabling a hostile peer to convince a client that divergent repos are synchronised. **Gate G3.2:** an audit assertion that no XOR/additive accumulator appears in any sync summary, plus an adversarial test that attempts to construct a colliding set digest for the chosen scheme and must fail. Scope it honestly: Lattice's ordered Merkle trees are already safe; the hazard lives only where sync negotiation digests a genuinely *unordered* set, such as "the set of checkpoint IDs I hold."

**What Lattice explicitly rejects.** No commutation as a primitive — Lattice never rewrites a change's byte-level representation to move it past another. No total algebraic merge — `LTX-MERGE` is a three-way procedure over trees with a conflict-value escape hatch, and is permitted to be a heuristic. No categorical soundness claim in any user-facing text; the claim Lattice makes is the testable one, that these properties hold over this generated corpus at this gate.

### Residual risk for Lattice

- **`entry_id` cannot survive a Git round trip, and this is new.** Git trees carry no file identity; renames are inferred heuristically at read time. So an `entry_id` minted in Lattice is dropped on export and unrecoverable on re-import — every `ltx sync` through a plain-Git teammate silently degrades exactly the identity that makes rename-vs-edit resolvable. The `git_commit_id ↔ checkpoint_id` correspondence table must therefore also persist `path ↔ entry_id` per exported commit, keyed by lens version, or the mechanism stolen in §1.4 stops working precisely for the teams the Git bridge exists to serve. **Gate G4.1**, retiered for feasibility: an in-process property tier of 10,000 randomised adopt → sync → foreign-push → re-sync round trips against a synthetic Git backend on every CI run, asserting zero duplicate checkpoints, byte-identical trees, and preserved `entry_id`; plus 100 nightly end-to-end round trips against a real `git` binary. Ten thousand real-`git` round trips per CI run is not a gate anyone will keep.
- **The Git bridge is the highest-risk subsystem, and Pijul is the proof.** Meunier's specific failure — an import that fails to recognise its own exported change and duplicates it under a new hash — reappears in Lattice as: `ltx sync` pushes lens-defined commits, a teammate pushes them onward, Lattice re-imports and cannot match them to the checkpoints that generated them. Lens-defined export makes this *harder* than a naive 1:1 export, because the mapping is many-to-one and lens definitions change between syncs. Without the correspondence table, Lattice inherits Pijul's exact bug.
- **Silent semantic-layer degradation makes merge non-deterministic across clients.** Two developers with different tree-sitter grammar versions produce different merge results from identical inputs, invisibly — which breaks G2.1's determinism assertions outright. Minimum mitigation: the grammar identity and version used for each structural merge is recorded in the resulting checkpoint's provenance, and fallback is visible in `ltx internals` even when quiet in normal output. "Silent" and "reproducible" are in tension; pick reproducible.
- **Worst cases, not averages, drive abandonment.** Pijul's own beta benchmarks — one Vim commit taking 5m16s to import (of 15,138), one Ruby commit ~25s to record and ~408s to apply (of 70,859), 913 of 52,664 CPython commits (1.7%) over 5s to record, and a no-op commit costing 48.7s because Pijul re-diffed all 3,706 files — show a mathematically sound system with pathological tails on ordinary repositories. Lattice routes all content through content-defined chunking and auto-snapshots continuously; the tail exposure is structurally similar, and the 48.7s no-op is the exact shape an auto-snapshotter hits. Gate `ltx record` and `ltx sync` on p99.9, not the mean.
- **Seven nouns is conceptual economy, not diagnosability, and Lattice gates neither.** Darcs had a *smaller* conceptual surface than Git and still lost on UX; GHC's complaint was diagnostic — you could not tell which two patches were conflicting. Lattice's conflict display must therefore carry the provenance of both sides (checkpoint, actor, change) as a first-class element, not behind `ltx internals`. There is currently no gate measuring whether a user can identify the origin of a conflict; propose one.

### Where the prior art says a Lattice target is unrealistic

- **"Lenses replace rebase" is over-claimed, and Darcs is the counterexample.** Darcs had exactly what lenses are meant to provide — order-independent cherry-pick, no need to rewrite history to reorder it — and shipped `darcs rebase` in 2.10.0 (19 April 2015) "to enable deep amending of history." Its own performance FAQ then recommends rebase as a way to *avoid* exponential merges. Lenses are read-path projections; they cannot amend a recorded change, split it, or drop a dependency. Revise the spec to enumerate which rebase use cases are served by lenses (presentation and publication shape), which by amendment-with-stable-ID (fixing a change in place), and which are unsupported in v1.
- **"Structural merge when the semantic layer covers the file" claims more than Pijul claims with a provably sound merge.** Pijul's answer to whether merged files have the correct semantics was a flat "No." A tree-sitter-derived structural merge is a heuristic over a regenerable index and can be *worse* than line merge when the parse is partial or the grammar mismatched. Frame it everywhere as reducing false conflicts, never as improving correctness, and require G2.1 to include cases where structural merge is asserted to *fall back* rather than guess.
- **Adoption realism.** Ten years in, Pijul draws under 2,000 crate downloads a quarter while Git-backed Jujutsu draws over 25,000 from a crate seven years younger. Nothing in Lattice's theory budget moves that number; `ltx sync` does. Keeping the Git bridge ahead of the semantic and agent layers is the single most defensible ordering decision in the spec, and the prior art says defend it against any pressure to reorder — including pressure from the agent layer, which is a weaker adoption lever than Git compatibility.
